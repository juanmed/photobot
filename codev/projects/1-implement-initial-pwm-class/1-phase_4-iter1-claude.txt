### Code Quality Assessment

**Locking strategy is correct and well-designed.** The plan specified: hold lock briefly in setters for `_pending` writes, and in `update()` only during the snapshot+clear, releasing before hardware I/O. The implementation follows this exactly:

- `set_duty_cycle()`: validates outside the lock (no slow path under lock), acquires only to write `_pending` — correct.
- `set_frequency()`: same pattern — validation and the `allow_runtime_freq_change` check happen outside the lock, only the `_pending` write is guarded — correct.
- `enable_channel()` / `disable_channel()`: lock protects the `enabled` flag mutation — correct. Note: `disable_channel()` writes `change_duty_cycle(0)` to hardware *outside* the lock (line 297), then acquires the lock to set `enabled=False` (line 298-299) — this is the right order to avoid holding the lock during I/O.
- `update()`: snapshot+clear under lock (lines 379-387), hardware writes outside lock (lines 390-406) — exactly as planned.

**One minor observation about `_timings` protection:** The plan says the lock should protect `_timings`, but `self._timings.append()` in `update()` (line 409) happens outside the lock. However, `deque.append()` is thread-safe in CPython due to the GIL, and `_timings` is only read by `get_timing_stats()` which also doesn't acquire the lock. This is acceptable — `deque` operations are atomic under CPython, and the plan's Phase 5 stress test will exercise this path. Not a bug, but worth noting.

**`fail_safe()` and `stop()` don't acquire the lock.** This is correct for their purpose — `fail_safe()` is a best-effort emergency path that must never deadlock, and it's called from within `update()` which may or may not already hold the lock at the time of a hardware failure. Making `fail_safe()` lock-free is the right design choice for a safety-critical method.
